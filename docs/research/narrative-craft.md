# Story games put the feeling in the player's hands

The games Olov named do not weave story into play by adding scenes between levels; they make the act of playing
carry it, and their makers describe the method plainly. Giant Sparrow built each *Edith Finch* story as play first
and decided whom it happened to only once the play had a beginning, a middle and an end ([Game
Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/creating-the-30-different-control-schemes-of-i-edith-finch-i-));
Night School set out to make a story game without cutscenes in which the player never loses control
([Player.One](https://www.player.one/oxenfree-creator-game-inspiration-ideas-121144)); Campo Santo put Henry's
marriage into a few minutes of text so that a radio could carry a relationship for the rest of *Firewatch*
([Slate](https://slate.com/culture/2016/02/the-video-game-firewatch-and-its-origins-in-1980s-text-adventures.html));
Josef Fares knew the ending of *Brothers* from the first day and designed its controls around it ([Game
Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2013/09/27/afterwords-brothers-a-tale-of-two-sons.aspx)); and
thatgamecompany drew one emotional arc for the whole of *Journey* before cutting it into levels ([Adventure
Gamers](https://adventuregamers.com/article/designing_journey)). Five techniques transfer to Elof's game: **set up
in the first minutes, as things to do and see, every fact the ending will need; give each chapter one moment in
which the verb is the feeling; deliver the mystery through one recurring device that sharpens on a schedule; keep
the family present at a distance through signs the player can answer; and end every chapter on a played beat and a
pause, with the tally last.** Two warnings are as well documented. *Firewatch*'s mystery resolved into something
smaller than it had promised, which a reviewer found anticlimactic
([GameCritics](https://gamecritics.com/steven-brown/firewatch-review/)), and, by its actor's account, its writer had
already found that an ending without its central relationship did not work
([TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/firewatch-ending-without-delilah/)). And nearly every celebrated emotional
opening or ending studied here (*Ori*, *Up*, *Brothers*, *Edith Finch*) runs on a death; Elof's game has to draw the
same weight from separation and reunion, which its plan already does with a lost carving, a lost crane chick and a
family that is near but out of reach. The evidence is good on what these developers did and why; how any of it lands
with a seven-year-old is this report's inference. All pages were read through search excerpts, as this session
cannot open them (last section).

## What Remains of Edith Finch builds each story as play, one feeling at a time

Ian Dallas started from the sublime, a moment that is beautiful and overwhelming at once
([GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/what-remains-of-edith-finch-dev-discusses-the-game/1100-6449846/)),
and says "all of these stories are really about people being overwhelmed in different ways" ([Digital
Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/gaming/what-remains-of-edith-finch-interview/)). The method was play first.
The team built isolated prototypes and did not think about story or character until it had "a clear beginning,
middle and end for the gameplay progression"; only then did it ask whom the event would happen to ([Game
Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/creating-the-30-different-control-schemes-of-i-edith-finch-i-)).
Each mechanic had to be new to players and still graspable without a tutorial ([GDC
Vault](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1025016/Weaving-13-Prototypes-into-1); [Game
Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/video-weaving-13-prototypes-into-1-game-for-i-what-remains-of-edith-finch-i-)),
and a second talk, by Giant Sparrow's Evan Rogers, sets the bar: each control scheme learned and mastered almost at
once, so as not to pull attention from the story, while gently setting its pace ([GDC
Vault](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1024948/Foolproofing-the-Controls-for-What)). The result is about thirty
control schemes on two sticks and one trigger, with no tutorial anywhere ([Game
Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/creating-the-30-different-control-schemes-of-i-edith-finch-i-)).

The stories show what that buys. In Calvin's, each stick is one of a boy's legs on a swing; designer Ben Esposito
prototyped it from nothing more than the idea of being a child on a swing ([Game
Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/creating-the-30-different-control-schemes-of-i-edith-finch-i-)), and
Dallas calls it his favourite because "it was so focused": by the time the player has worked out how it feels, it is
over
([GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/what-remains-of-edith-finch-dev-discusses-the-game/1100-6449846/)). In
Lewis's, one hand cuts fish in a cannery while the other steers a daydream that slowly takes over the screen, so the
player's attention is split as his is ([Digital
Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/gaming/what-remains-of-edith-finch-interview/)); it went through several
versions and was, with Gregory's, the story most likely to be cut ([PC
Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/the-making-of-what-remains-of-edith-finch/)). Walter's story riffs on *Doctor Who*'s
Weeping Angels and *The Prisoner* and is gradually revealed to be a model railway ([PC
Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/the-making-of-what-remains-of-edith-finch/)). Molly's, the first story played, turns
a hungry girl into a cat, an owl, a shark and a sea monster
([Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/pc/what-remains-of-edith-finch-story-and-ending-explained/11/), an explainer).
Gregory's, a baby's bath, went through many versions; one ended with the water rising, which Dallas found "too much
of a gut punch", and the shipped one lets the player go under with control, which he says gives the forward momentum
of a journey instead of ending with a curtain rising in front of the player ([God is a
Geek](https://godisageek.com/2017/04/interview-with-ian-dallas-creative-director-on-what-remains-of-edith-finch/)).

The house is the frame. Each story is entered from the dead person's bedroom, and Dallas says the bedrooms exist to
ease the player into an intense story and out of it again, with a period of relative calm and then time to pause
([God is a
Geek](https://godisageek.com/2017/04/interview-with-ian-dallas-creative-director-on-what-remains-of-edith-finch/)).
Edith's spoken narration also appears in the world as 3D text, which the team found points the eye, highlights what
a player might miss and gives an abandoned house movement ([interview with
Dallas](https://bounthavy.com/en/interview-with-ian-dallas-on-what-remains-of-edith-finch/)). Endings were the hard
part: some prototypes were dropped because no satisfying ending could be found, and whether the family curse is real
is left to the player ([Gaming
Trend](https://gamingtrend.com/interviews/interview-ian-dallas-talks-about-ambiguity-cut-features-and-magic-in-what-remains-of-edith-finch/)).
The final reveal is planted from the start: Edith is pregnant, which a player can discover by looking down, and
Dallas liked that players could find it themselves ([PC
Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/the-making-of-what-remains-of-edith-finch/)); the last scene shows that her journal
was written for her son, who has been reading it at her grave ([Edith Finch
Wiki](https://what-remains-of-edith-finch.fandom.com/wiki/Edith_Finch)).

## Firewatch spends ten minutes on the past, then lets a radio carry the relationship

Sean Vanaman wrote the prologue because the team needed "to inject all this knowledge about Henry into every player
in the first five to 10 minutes", and made it a small text game in which the player is Henry, so that nobody had to
be told who he was
([Slate](https://slate.com/culture/2016/02/the-video-game-firewatch-and-its-origins-in-1980s-text-adventures.html)).
It is choice-based text about Henry's marriage, interleaved with short played scenes of his arrival in the woods,
and it lets the player personalise him at once ([Emily
Short](https://emshort.blog/2016/02/12/firewatch-campo-santo/), criticism). One critical reading is that whatever is
chosen, Henry fails his wife and runs away, so the player arrives at the tower already sharing his guilt ([Press
Start](https://press-start.com.au/features/2016/02/11/firewatch-explanation-and-analysis/), criticism).

After that, the relationship lives in a walkie-talkie: Henry's supervisor Delilah exists only as a voice on the
radio ([GamesBeat](https://venturebeat.com/2015/12/22/crafting-relationships-through-radio-in-firewatch/)). The
dialogue system was built to put "barks in the hands of the player": he radios about something, the game strikes up
a conversation about it, and he can interrupt at any time ([Game
Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/come-to-gdc-2017-and-hear-how-i-firewatch-i-s-unique-dialog-system-works);
[GDC Vault](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1024415/Do-You-Copy-Dialog-System)). Vanaman expected each player to hear
only part of what was recorded, and said "the stuff you do hear will completely define your story"
([GamesBeat](https://venturebeat.com/2015/12/22/crafting-relationships-through-radio-in-firewatch/)). Choices are
mostly implicit: Chris Remo describes a food cache that was once an explicit choice and became a passing remark from
Delilah, whose later reaction is the reward or the punishment
([TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/firewatch-linear-choices/)). At GDC 2014 Vanaman described the aim as courting
the player, and named the ways it fails: bad dialogue, or too few things to find and interact with ([Game
Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/why-game-design-should-feel-like-taking-your-player-out-on-a-date)).
Time is cut into days with title cards, and the game skips days and weeks between them, so the summer is felt
passing in jumps
([Hoeschen](https://medium.com/@roryhoeschen/watching-the-summer-pass-the-temporal-construction-of-firewatch-e89fb19d74c2),
criticism).

The ending is the cautionary part. The mystery the two build together turns out to have a small, sad explanation,
and one reviewer wrote that "the true finale was more than a little anticlimactic"
([GameCritics](https://gamecritics.com/steven-brown/firewatch-review/)). An essay that Campo Santo published argues
that the anticlimax is the point, since the conspiracy was Henry's way of running from his life, and notes that
Delilah never tries to meet him in person ([Campo Santo
Quarterly](https://quarterly.camposanto.com/the-end-of-firewatch-8a7d334a9586), an essay by Duncan Fyfe). The
developers' own course shows where the line lies. According to Delilah's actor, the first recorded version had
Delilah go cold and silent for the last third of the game, and Vanaman came back saying "it doesn't work and we have
to have Delilah", so the ending was re-recorded
([TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/firewatch-ending-without-delilah/)).

## Oxenfree never stops the walk to talk, and gives the ghost story to a radio

Adam Hines and Sean Krankel wanted a game in which conversation never stops and what the player says steers the
story, and found it "very, very hard" ([Game
Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-i-oxenfree-i-s-narrative-unfolds-like-a-free-flowing-conversation)).
Krankel's summary of the premise is "a story game with no cutscenes, a game that never takes control away from the
player", with the dialogue system imagined as a physics toy
([Player.One](https://www.player.one/oxenfree-creator-game-inspiration-ideas-121144)). His two rules were to stop
taking control away for cutscenes and to put the choices as close to the character as possible, and they drove the
camera, the art direction and the pacing ([The
Koalition](https://thekoalition.com/2015/oxenfree-fear-in-frequency-interview-with-sean-krankel)). At GDC 2017 he
said the team kept judging the game by two questions, why the player is playing at all and "What is the player doing
while we have them?" ([Game
Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/video-building-game-mechanics-to-elevate-narrative-in-i-oxenfree-i-)).
Alex says nothing unless the player makes her ([80
Level](https://80.lv/articles/the-gdc-talk-about-game-mechanics)). Her options rise as bubbles over her head while
others are still talking and fade if not taken, so interrupting, waiting and silence are all choices
([GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/interrupt-dialogue-in-oxenfree-makes-conversations-feel-natural/)).

The opening is a lesson in disguise. On the ferry, Alex walks the deck with Jonas and Ren while the talk runs, and
the radio, the game's main tool, is introduced there, when Ren gives her a frequency to tune to
([TrueAchievements](https://www.trueachievements.com/game/OXENFREE/walkthrough/3), walkthrough). The same radio then
carries the ghost story. Krankel loved how *Poltergeist* uses "the breakdown of communication as something scary,
but also really playful"
([Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/the-poltergeist-inspired-oxenfree-looks-like-an-indie-gaming-essential-919/)),
and the team wanted that film's tone rather than blood and jump scares
([Yahoo](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/oxenfree-80s-movies-real-history-170000890.html)). The supernatural
arrives through that device and escalates: anomalies found by tuning at particular places, a station whose clues
lead to hidden letters about the island's past and rifts opened by tuning ([Oxenfree
Wiki](https://oxenfree.fandom.com/wiki/Radio); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxenfree)), then time loops
that replay a scene with twisted outcomes ([WellPlayed](https://www.well-played.com.au/oxenfree-review/)) and ghosts
who speak through Alex's friends ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxenfree)). The epilogue shows what the
night changed: Alex tells what became of each friend, depending on the player's choices
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxenfree)), and Krankel says the aim was for players to feel they had
"forever permanently changed Alex and her friends' lives for better or worse"
([Player.One](https://www.player.one/oxenfree-creator-endings-sequel-game-121233)).

## Ori earns love before loss, in a prologue that took eight months

*Ori and the Blind Forest* opens with what one review calls "a ten-minute, mostly non-interactive sequence"
([GameCritics](https://gamecritics.com/mike-suskie/ori-and-the-blind-forest-review/)). With minimal play, the player
steers Ori and then Naru in turn through the small acts of a shared life, gathering fruit and building bridges,
until the forest withers and Naru goes out alone to look for food
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ori_and_the_Blind_Forest)); a narrator, the Spirit Tree, speaks in an
invented language ([Ori Wiki](https://oriandtheblindforest.fandom.com/wiki/Ori_and_the_Blind_Forest)). Thomas Mahler
says the prologue took "a whopping 8 months", with many moments when it did not yet feel right
([Ori](https://www.orithegame.com/qa-with-thomas-mahler-celebrating-8-years-of-ori-the-game/)). He explains its
effect through the audience's own memories: a mother starving and the loss of a loved one make people think of
losses of their own, and the composer Gareth Coker says the same of those who cry at it
([GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps-story-goes-in-a-diff/1100-6474508/)).
The tool is animation: Mahler rejects motion capture because hand animation can convey mood, personality and
intention "completely wordlessly"
([GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/i-think-mo-cap-is-dumb-moon-studios-thomas-mahler-on-the-art-and-science-behind-ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps/)).
For the sequel he found the bond harder to build, because Ori could not be given another parent at the start
([Console
Creatures](https://www.consolecreatures.com/interview-with-thomas-mahler-about-ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps/)).

## Brothers puts its climax in the player's hands

Josef Fares knew from the first day how *Brothers* would end and what its controls would be: the left hand for the
older brother and the right for the younger, simplified early to one action button each ([Game
Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2013/09/27/afterwords-brothers-a-tale-of-two-sons.aspx)). The
ending is built on that mapping. The game opens at the mother's grave, with the younger brother remembering how she
drowned while he could not save her
([TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/brothers-a-tale-of-two-sons-remake-review/)). Throughout, he is the small one
who squeezes through gaps, and he cannot swim, so he rides on his brother's back in water; after the older brother
dies, the trigger that always belonged to him is what makes the younger one paddle across
([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/brothers-tale-of-two-sons-remake-ending-older-younger-1851302128)). Fares describes the
intent as the player losing "one physical hand", so that the absence is felt in the hands ([Game
Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2013/09/27/afterwords-brothers-a-tale-of-two-sons.aspx)). The
characters speak an invented language based on Arabic, which Fares says makes players read body language more
closely ([Adventure Gamers](https://adventuregamers.com/articles/view/33389); [Game
Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2013/09/27/afterwords-brothers-a-tale-of-two-sons.aspx)).

## Journey maps one emotional arc, keeps the goal in view and makes help the mechanic

Jenova Chen says "we always start with emotion and work everything around it" ([The
Escapist](https://www.escapistmagazine.com/d-i-c-e-2013-jenova-chen-journey-is-a-game-about-strangers/)). For
*Journey* the team mapped a hero's journey from birth to death across the whole game, set an emotional range for
each part, and only then chose the play and story of each segment ([Adventure
Gamers](https://adventuregamers.com/article/designing_journey); [Game
Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/video-designing-a-new-emotional-experience-in-i-journey-i-)). Testing
shaped the curve. The climb through the snow, where the scarf shrinks and the traveller slogs on until collapsing,
was once three times as long and was cut after testers gave up on it; Chen's measure was "three minutes is enough
time to walk to your death" ([Adventure Gamers](https://adventuregamers.com/article/designing_journey)). The joyful
flight that follows was opened up instead of kept on rails, with the sand-surfing of earlier levels brought back for
a moment ([talk
notes](https://journey-archive.fandom.com/wiki/User_blog:JAlbor/GDC_2013:_Designing_Journey_with_Jenova_Chen), a
fan's summary). The goal is set without a word: from the first dune the player sees the mountain with light at its
peak, and the title appears
([CGMagazine](https://www.cgmagonline.com/articles/features/journey-its-all-about-that-first-step/), criticism).
Each area ends at a shrine where a white-robed figure shows part of the story in pictures
([StrategyWiki](https://strategywiki.org/wiki/Journey_(2012))).

The companion is the other half. There are no names, no chat and no lobby, and Chen wanted the interaction to be
"about exchange of emotions or feelings, rather than exchange of blows"
([MCV](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/jenova-chen-reveals-secrets-of-journey-design/)). An early idea of lifting
each other over obstacles was dropped when players used contact to push each other into danger ([The
Escapist](https://www.escapistmagazine.com/d-i-c-e-2013-jenova-chen-journey-is-a-game-about-strangers/)). What
remained is one chirp, which also recharges the companion's scarf, so calling is helping, and in the snow staying
close keeps both warm ([Journey Wiki](https://journey.fandom.com/wiki/Chirp); [Journey
Wiki](https://journey.fandom.com/wiki/Snow)). The companion's name appears only in the credits ([Video Game
Positioning](https://videogamepositioning.substack.com/p/journey-stripped-everything-and-owned), commentary). Robin
Hunicke's early brief included "Somewhere you feel very small" ([Game
Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/in-depth-hunicke-and-chen-talk-tgc-s-intriguing-new-i-journey-i-)).

## Six briefer cases: Up, Pixar's story rules, A Short Hike, Spiritfarer, Lost in Play and GRIS

**Up.** The opening montage became wordless when story artist Ronnie del Carmen suggested letting the music do the
talking; Pete Docter, who had overwritten the opening with 30 to 40 minutes of material, kept cutting and found "the
less we had the more emotional it felt" ([The
Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2022/06/14/movies/up-first-10-minutes-making-of)). His team tied the house to the
marriage and to an unfinished promise so that the audience would care about it ([The
Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2022/06/14/movies/up-first-10-minutes-making-of)), and the film pays the promise
off at its climax with the adventure book from the montage ([The Story
Department](https://www.thestorydepartment.com/up-the-moments-that-never-come/), analysis).

**Pixar's story rules.** Emma Coats's list includes the story spine (once upon a time, every day, one day, because
of that, until finally), the advice to work out the ending before the middle, and the rule "Coincidences to get
characters into trouble are great; coincidences to get them out of it are cheating."
([Coats](https://files.commons.gc.cuny.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/4120/files/2018/08/Pixars-22-Rules-of-Storytelling.pdf)).

**A Short Hike.** Adam Robinson-Yu says the game's ending, at the top of the climb, is about how a relationship with
one's parents changes as one grows up, and that he wanted players to leave with a warm feeling
([GamePilgrim](https://gamepilgrim.com/2020/02/26/developer-interview-adam-robinson-yu/)).

**Spiritfarer.** Care is a verb: Stella can hug her passengers, and Nicolas Guérin says "it felt like you needed to
have a physical connection to lift someone's spirit" ([Origin
Story](https://www.originstory.show/episodes/nick-guerin)). Each spirit eventually says it is ready, and the player
must take it to the Everdoor ([Screen Rant](https://screenrant.com/spiritfarer-how-keep-people-on-boat-make-stay/)),
after which a constellation in its likeness joins the night sky ([TV
Tropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/VideoGame/Spiritfarer)).

**Lost in Play.** Happy Juice tells two siblings' make-believe adventure without comprehensible dialogue, through
gibberish, gestures, pictures and sounds ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_in_Play)), with the
children based on a founder's own ([80
Level](https://80.lv/articles/happy-juice-games-production-of-animation-quest-games)); "We've all watched our kids
get totally immersed playing make-believe games," says Yuval Markovich
([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=n4w6zydm)).

**GRIS.** Nomada wanted no fail states and no dialogue or text, built the arc from black and white towards as many
colours as possible, and wanted an open ending ([Red Bull](https://www.redbull.com/us-en/gris-preview-interview)).

### An opening re-cut along these lines

Inference on top of the plan's prologue (§3.4) and the overhaul's eight beats; additions are marked new.

1. New: sound before picture, a knife in wood, then Pappa's hands finishing the ghost at the kitchen table in the
   morning sun, the blade always moving away from his body. Full-size Elof can walk round the table from the first
   second and blows the last shavings off with him.
2. New: the morning, 60 to 90 seconds of play. Each family member is busy with their own object; Mamma's "Den får du
   öppna ikväll" becomes a picture bubble of the moon over the bag; Bertil's hand sneaks towards the bag and Elof
   pulls it back (planned).
3. The shelf with Pappa's figures and the empty first place (planned), and new, the fridge drawing as a second seed.
4. Pappa hands him the brush: *Måla ögonen*, the blink, and the ghost's look at the empty place and then at the bag
   before it takes it (planned).
5. The freeze joke and the chase at full size, with hints only if needed (planned).
6. New: the tear; the candy trickles out, the star and the golden geléhallon glint, and the camera holds a beat on
   the golden one inside the running bag.
7. The star, *POFF*, and the camera dropping with him beside the family (as the overhaul stages it).
8. New in part: worried faces close by, Pappa kneeling with "Följ godisspåret, Elof. Vi är nära dig hela tiden.",
   then the giant hands taking the morning's objects as the family sets off.
9. New: the title over the garden, with the mountain and the old pine on the horizon.

### A chapter close in six beats

Also inference. It replaces the cut from the last action to "Kapitel 1 klart!" and the rows of candy (plan §3.4),
and gives the overhaul's chapter-end handoff a place to live.

1. The last beat is played: Elof walks out of the chapter's final moment himself.
2. A look back: the camera rises to show the ground he crossed in one picture while the chapter's theme resolves;
   three to five seconds, input still live, nothing to collect.
3. The ghost, ahead, looks back and shows its next bubble: the question that carries into the next chapter.
4. A sign of the family on its larger route: the headlamps, a giant hand on a railing, Moa with her map.
5. Moa's map: her crayon draws the path just travelled, the sweets are dots along it and the stickers small
   drawings; the count is a picture, and missed sweets are not shown as gaps.
6. The next chapter's card: the Saturday time as a sun and a clock on the map, then the new light, and play. About
   20 to 30 seconds in all, skippable after the first time.

## What to avoid for a seven-year-old

- **Scenes he cannot play or skip.** Night School's rule was never to take control away
  ([Player.One](https://www.player.one/oxenfree-creator-game-inspiration-ideas-121144)), and *Ori*'s much-praised
  opening is mostly non-interactive for ten minutes
  ([GameCritics](https://gamecritics.com/mike-suskie/ori-and-the-blind-forest-review/)). Inference: for a boy who
  plays games made for eleven-year-olds, anything longer than the 6 to 10 second memories should give him something
  to do, and every scene should be skippable on a replay.
- **Story that depends on reading.** *Firewatch*'s prologue is text ([Emily
  Short](https://emshort.blog/2016/02/12/firewatch-campo-santo/)), and *Edith Finch* doubles its spoken narration as
  words in the world ([interview with
  Dallas](https://bounthavy.com/en/interview-with-ian-dallas-on-what-remains-of-edith-finch/)). Elof's game has no
  voices, so any words would carry the story alone; every beat must work with the bubbles ignored (plan pillar 3),
  and the bubbles stay optional.
- **Choices on a clock.** Oxenfree's options fade if not taken
  ([GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/interrupt-dialogue-in-oxenfree-makes-conversations-feel-natural/)). No
  choice, share or answer in Elof's game should vanish while he thinks.
- **Death and gut punches.** Dallas changed Gregory's ending because the first was too much ([God is a
  Geek](https://godisageek.com/2017/04/interview-with-ian-dallas-creative-director-on-what-remains-of-edith-finch/)),
  and *Ori*, *Brothers* and *Up* each turn on a death
  ([GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps-story-goes-in-a-diff/1100-6474508/);
  [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/brothers-tale-of-two-sons-remake-ending-older-younger-1851302128); [The
  Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2022/06/14/movies/up-first-10-minutes-making-of)). Draw weight from separation
  and reunion; keep the figure alone in the crack brief, and resolve it within minutes.
- **Guilt.** One reading of *Firewatch*'s prologue is that it makes the player share Henry's failures ([Press
  Start](https://press-start.com.au/features/2016/02/11/firewatch-explanation-and-analysis/)). Nobody in Elof's
  story should be to blame: the tear, the star and the gust stay accidents, and memory 4 keeps showing Pappa
  reaching for the figure rather than dwelling on how it fell.
- **Endings left open.** GRIS wanted an open ending ([Red
  Bull](https://www.redbull.com/us-en/gris-preview-interview)), *Edith Finch* leaves its curse to the player
  ([Gaming
  Trend](https://gamingtrend.com/interviews/interview-ian-dallas-talks-about-ambiguity-cut-features-and-magic-in-what-remains-of-edith-finch/))
  and a critic reads Oxenfree's epilogue as a loop
  ([Haywire](https://haywiremag.com/features/oxenfree-and-the-poetics-of-the-inexpressible/)). For a seven-year-old,
  close every thread and state the answer once in a picture and one bubble, as the plan's gated reveal does.
- **A mystery that fizzles or misleads.** *Firewatch*'s red herrings made its small answer feel smaller
  ([GameCritics](https://gamecritics.com/steven-brown/firewatch-review/)); every clue here should be honest, and the
  answer bigger than the theft.
- **A tally at the emotional peak.** No source addresses score cards directly; the inference comes from Dallas's
  ease-out ([God is a
  Geek](https://godisageek.com/2017/04/interview-with-ian-dallas-creative-director-on-what-remains-of-edith-finch/)).
  Numbers come after the pause, on the map, and never as missed candy.
- **Too much at once.** Lewis's two-handed story was among the hardest to finish ([PC
  Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/the-making-of-what-remains-of-edith-finch/)); the memories stay watch-only, or have
  at most one tap.

## Where the evidence is thin

This session's network policy refused every source host tried (Wikipedia, Game Developer, GDC Vault, YouTube and the
studios' own sites answered 403 at the proxy), so no page was opened directly: everything above was read through the
web-search tool's excerpts of the linked pages, and the quotations are as those excerpts gave them, to be checked on
Olov's computer before reuse. The GDC talks (Dallas 2018, Rogers on *Edith Finch*'s controls, Krankel 2017, Ewing
and Armstrong 2017, Chen 2013) are known through session descriptions and press write-ups, not watched. The best
unread primary source is *Firewatch*'s developer commentary, a free ebook
([GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/firewatchs-funny-and-thoughtful-developer-commentary-is-now-a-free-ebook/);
[transcript](https://projectfirewatch.com/cdn/FirewatchAudioTour.pdf)), which covers the prologue and the ending in
the developers' own words.

No developer statement was found on *Firewatch*'s day cards, *Oxenfree*'s pacing of its anomalies or how *Ori*'s
prologue teaches its controls; those passages rest on wikis, walkthroughs and reviews, marked as such. Claims that
could not be pinned to a page were left out, such as a link between *Up* and Ori's prologue, which reviewers draw
but no developer statement found here does. The sources also disagree: reviewers criticise *Firewatch*'s anticlimax
and an essay Campo Santo published defends it; for adults it is a choice, for a seven-year-old the evidence points
one way. No source here studies children; every application to Elof is inference, and his first play (H4) is the
test.
