# Great platformers coil lines into places

The best platformers are guided lines, and their makers say so: Ori's director calls *Blind Forest* "a lot more linear" than its sequel ([Film Stories](https://filmstories.co.uk/gaming/ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps-interview-making-a-masterpiece/)), a mapping of the first *Zelda* found its critical path "almost entirely linear" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/learning-from-the-masters-level-design-in-i-the-legend-of-zelda-i-)), and Nintendo rebuilt 3D Mario so that "you head to the right or advance deeper in to definitely reach a goal" ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/3ds/super-mario-3d-land/0/1/)). What separates them from a corridor is a short grammar laid over that line, the same from Mario to Cocoon: every few minutes one idea is taught safely, developed, twisted and shown off; the line climbs, dips under itself and returns to a landmark; things are seen before they can be reached; places change after the player has crossed them; and finishing stays easy while optional collectibles carry the skill and the curiosity. Elof's chapters should therefore keep the left-to-right candy trail and change what hangs on it: **three arcs of four steps per chapter, one rule of the place, six to eight short pockets that let out forward, a landmark the trail comes back to, one event that changes screens already crossed, four special sweets split between the brave and the curious, and an exciting sequence in which he follows the ghost instead of fleeing a hazard**. The most concrete gap is the lace: in the greybox files of the four story chapters every swing ring hangs within ten Elof lengths under the garden deck, so the game's signature verb, the part of *Unravel* that reviewers enjoyed most, is taught and then dropped for three chapters. The kindness rules already in the plan (the glitter bubble, big candy, things on rails, a helper only on call) are what the evidence recommends, and the risk it points to is the opposite one: *Kirby's Epic Yarn* stopped being fun for its novice tester after two stages while it was only easy and pretty, so a child who plays games made for older children needs things to do well, not things to lose. The evidence is strong on what designers do and why it works for players in general; it is thin on seven-year-olds, where nearly every recommendation is an inference from usability and wayfinding studies and from what developers say about beginners.

## The admired games are guided lines, so linearity is not the fault

None of the games studied here is the open playground it feels like. Thomas Mahler says "Blind Forest was a lot more linear" than its sequel, and a long analysis of both Ori games observes that "pretty much every area in the Ori games is entered through one intended path", which is what lets Moon Studios craft each challenge around the abilities it knows the player has ([Film Stories](https://filmstories.co.uk/gaming/ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps-interview-making-a-masterpiece/); [mossbag](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=So9E1egsaHU&t=3062s)). A critic of the genre concludes that such levels "are actually still designed linearly, just with some vestigial bits grafted to them and coiled around these pivot points" ([Unwinnable](https://unwinnable.com/2018/11/28/metroidvania/)). Mike Stout mapped the dungeons of the first *Zelda*, found the critical path "almost entirely linear" although players remember them as open, and drew the rule that a designer gets "the feel of non-linear level design by taking a linear path and adding short offshoots" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/learning-from-the-masters-level-design-in-i-the-legend-of-zelda-i-)). Nintendo chose the line on purpose: Satoru Iwata said that free roaming "carries with it the problem of not knowing where to go and getting lost", so the courses of *Super Mario 3D Land* were designed so that "you head to the right or advance deeper in to definitely reach a goal" ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/3ds/super-mario-3d-land/0/1/)). Team Asobi considered an open world for *Astro Bot* and chose levels because that structure gave "the most control over the game's variety" ([GamesRadar+](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/platformer/astro-bot-almost-went-open-world-but-opted-for-80-planets-instead-because-that-led-to-the-most-control-over-the-games-variety/)).

A corridor, then, is not a line. It is a line with nothing smaller than the chapter inside it, nothing to come back to, nothing that can be declined and only one tempo. The sources name five things a corridor lacks: **a unit of a few minutes** in which one idea is taught, developed, twisted and mastered; **a second pass** over ground already seen, by a loop, a landmark or a change in the place; **an optional layer** that carries the skill and the curiosity while finishing stays easy; **a density of small reactions**; and **contrast** between thinking, flowing, resting and one loud moment. The dissenting voices point the same way. Mark Brown found that in Ori "the Metroidvania structure mostly just leads to backtracking and confusion, rather than navigational joy" ([Game Maker's Toolkit](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VQ_KrRq4UiA&t=430s)), and a study of patterns in more than thirty 2D games warns that branching can give "a false sense of choice" when most paths must be walked anyway ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/level-design-patterns-in-2d-games)). The lesson is to borrow the devices and leave the map.

Elof's chapters are corridors in exactly this sense, and not because they run left to right. A check of the greybox files of the four story chapters on this worktree (4 October 2026) shows the pattern. The format describes the ground as "one open line, from left to right" (`src/sim/types.ts`), and in Gården each toy stands at one station: all four swing rings, the two on the main path and the two extra nails of the challenge chain, hang between x = 54 and x = 64 of a chapter whose goal is at x = 210; the two shaving curls on rails stand together between x = 133 and x = 141; and the paper plane closes the chapter. **No swing ring hangs anywhere in Granskogen, Myren or Berget**, where the lace only pulls things and is climbed; the extra village chapter after the story has one. Granskogen comes closest to what the sources describe, because its cones are met three times with three meanings: as something to climb, as an avalanche to jump, and as the weight on Pappa's seesaw. The overhaul of 4 October also gave each chapter one optional return loop (a paper pocket under Gården's shaving bridge, the root door in Granskogen, Mamma's boardwalk in Myren, a lace down from Berget's cairn), which is the right kind of device; each is a single loop beside a line whose toys still stand at single stations. The rest of this report explains why that sequence is the right instinct, how to give every chapter one like it, and how far the line can be coiled before a seven-year-old loses it.

## Twelve case studies, one grammar: what each game does and why it works

Developer statements carry the most weight in what follows. Walkthroughs establish what is in a level, and critics supply the reason where developers are silent; the last section lists what could not be verified.

### Super Mario builds each course on one idea in four steps, and makes coins a promise

Koichi Hayashida, director of *Super Mario 3D Land*, says "it's really important to decide on a core concept in level design" and describes a course as four steps: "First, you have to learn how to use that gameplay mechanic, and then the stage will offer you a slightly more complicated scenario in which you have to use it. And then the next step is something crazy happens that makes you think about it in a way you weren't expecting. And then you get to demonstrate, finally, what sort of mastery you've gained over it." His reason is that acquiring a skill "often appears very similar to the way that a narrative can develop", so one course gives the player a small story about getting better ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-structure-of-fun-learning-from-i-super-mario-3d-land-i-s-director)). The older Miyamoto rule is the same idea in three parts: "First there's a basic instance, then a developed instance, and then an advanced, combined instance. He often said, 'Use one element three times.'" ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/mario25th/2/2/)). **The twist is a combination of known things, never a new button**, and a critic times the whole cycle at "about five minutes flat" ([MCV/Develop](https://www.mcvuk.com/development/video-nintendos-level-design-secrets-in-four-steps)). The often-quoted "safe environment" for the first step is that critic's wording, not Hayashida's ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/what-made-super-mario-3d-world-so-great-1691916400)).

Mario teaches and guides by arrangement. In World 1-1 the mushroom rebounds off a pipe and the block overhead stops a panicked jump, "so that whatever you do, you'll get the mushroom", and the level was built last, on the team's principle "to move easier features to the beginning" once beginners had found the later worlds too hard ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/0/3/); [Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/1/4/)). Coins are an instruction the player wants to follow. They "show you the route to take" and are placed "so if you just jump naturally, you can get them", and they carry a promise, in Iwata's words "If I jump to get the coins, there's no way anything bad will happen", which is why Takashi Tezuka had "an enemy right after a coin" removed as "mean placement" ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/mario25th/3/4/); [Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/2/6/); [Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/1/5/); [Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/3ds/super-mario-3d-land/0/5/)). The goal is kept easy and the collectibles carry the difficulty: "All of the courses can be cleared without dashing or wall jumping, but you can't get Star Coins without doing those things" ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/2/1/)), and Miyamoto said in 1990 that for a game players come back to and explore, "making the levels easier is a pre-requisite" ([shmuplations](https://shmuplations.com/supermarioworld/)). Nintendo's own course-making lessons state what makes a secret fair: a visible clue for every hidden thing, no reward shown where it cannot be reached, no place that traps the player and no punishment for looking ([Yamamura's Dojo](https://www.mariowiki.com/Yamamura%27s_Dojo)). *Super Mario Bros. Wonder* turned the twist into a system, with "at least one element in each course that would surprise or delight players" and a course that "transforms without players having to warp", and kept the ordinary rules trustworthy by giving the changed state its own look ([Nintendo](https://www.nintendo.com/us/whatsnew/ask-the-developer-vol-11-super-mario-bros-wonder-part-1/)).

### Tropical Freeze gives a level one place that changes, and two collectibles for two tempers

Retro Studios learned the Nintendo method on *Donkey Kong Country Returns*, where "everything in that level is there for a reason", and applied it to levels that a magazine preview clocked at "around 10 minutes" on a first attempt, close to one of Elof's chapters ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/donkey-kong-country-returns/0/1/); [My Nintendo News](https://mynintendonews.com/2014/01/14/the-official-nintendo-magazine-reveals-plenty-of-donkey-kong-country-tropical-freeze-info/)). In Mark Brown's reading each stage ties two or three mechanics to one theme, puts "an intermission of sorts" in the middle and uses "two or three" checkpoints ([Game Maker's Toolkit](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JqHcE6B4OP4&t=509s)). The place itself carries the twist. In Sawmill Thrill a mine cart becomes a log boat on a water slide and is sawn back into a cart ([Super Mario Wiki](https://www.mariowiki.com/Sawmill_Thrill)); the storm of one level leaves the next one burning, so that a water sack becomes that level's single tool ([Super Mario Wiki](https://www.mariowiki.com/Scorch_%27n%27_Torch)); and the moving background "sometimes provides hints to what you're going to encounter next" ([My Nintendo News](https://mynintendonews.com/2014/01/14/the-official-nintendo-magazine-reveals-plenty-of-donkey-kong-country-tropical-freeze-info/)). **Its two collectibles serve two temperaments.** KONG letters sit in view on the main line, are lost again if the player fails before the next checkpoint, and ask for nerve; Puzzle Pieces sit off the line, are kept the moment they are touched, and ask for looking ([Super Mario Wiki](https://www.mariowiki.com/KONG_Letters); [Super Mario Wiki](https://www.mariowiki.com/Puzzle_Piece_%28Donkey_Kong_Country_series%29)). Brown puts it as "puzzle pieces are about exploration and so are hidden in secret areas; the KONG letters are more about platforming prowess and so they're very visible but they ask you to put yourself at risk to grab them" ([Game Maker's Toolkit](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JqHcE6B4OP4&t=556s)). No first-hand Retro statement on that split was found, and one critic complains that the hidden bonus rooms "repeated a lot" ([Game-Wisdom](https://game-wisdom.com/analysis/donkey-kong-country-tropical-freeze)).

### Kirby and Yoshi show what a gentle game owes the player in return

The most useful single story for a game without death is *Kirby's Epic Yarn*. Its first build had almost no way to fail, and the tester who "isn't very good at action games" enjoyed the atmosphere "for about the first two stages" and no longer; Nintendo's Matsumiya said the game lacked "what every action game needs: tension over enemies, traps, and how you can overcome them". Iwata's conclusion was that "when you try to make something fun simply by lowering the difficulty and using a neat atmosphere, that fun wears off". The overcorrection ("thorns everywhere") was as bad. The fix kept the rule that the game never ends and added "individual mechanisms and contrivances that would be fun for players to overcome", plus beads that scatter on a hit, so that players "really feel regret when you run into enemies and a strong sense of achievement when you clear a level without any mistakes"; it was tuned in weekly sessions of watching one expert and one novice play ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/kirbysepicyarn/0/1/); [Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/kirbysepicyarn/0/2/)). *Kirby and the Forgotten Land* repeated the lesson: a version "easy enough that even a three-year-old child could play" lost its "thrills and sense of adventure", and the answers were quiet areas for less skilled players, an optional harder "Wild Mode" in place of an easy mode, a camera that "automatically displays landmarks in the direction you're supposed to travel", and leniency such as treating Kirby "as if he's already landed" when the button is pressed just above the ground ([Nintendo](https://www.nintendo.com/us/whatsnew/ask-the-developer-vol-4-kirby-and-the-forgotten-land-part-1/); [Nintendo](https://www.nintendo.com/us/whatsnew/ask-the-developer-vol-4-kirby-and-the-forgotten-land-part-2/); [Nintendo](https://www.nintendo.com/us/whatsnew/ask-the-developer-vol-4-kirby-and-the-forgotten-land-part-4/)). Yoshi puts the pushback in collecting, "if you try to get all the collectibles, you'll find it pretty challenging. It'll push back" ([Nintendo World Report](https://www.nintendoworldreport.com/interview/37850/interview-with-takashi-tezuka-on-yoshis-woolly-world-mario-maker)), beside a Mellow Mode that can be switched on or off at almost any point ([Super Mario Wiki](https://www.mariowiki.com/Mellow_Mode)). **This works because the help is chosen and can be dropped.** Help that arrives uninvited is resented, as Miyamoto found when a hint block shown from the start made him feel "I don't need this!" ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/0/5/)).

### Astro Bot runs two clocks: thirty seconds and ten minutes

Nicolas Doucet states the density target outright: "Every 30 seconds we want there to be something you can talk about, something people will remember" ([GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/how-astro-bot-went-from-tech-demo-to-playstation-superstar)), beside "a renewed experience every 10 minutes or so" ([Push Square](https://www.pushsquare.com/news/2024/06/astro-bot-is-the-biggest-game-team-asobi-has-ever-made)) and a tempo of jumps, punches and coins that "needs to feel almost like a melody" ([One More Game](https://onemoregame.ph/2024/09/astro-bot-director-nicolas-doucet-interview/)). The thirty-second item need not be a mechanic; his own example is a gag in which punching a tree shakes loose a little figure, three apples and a rabbit. Variety is protected by a rule against repeating: "usually one power-up appears twice", a third time only in a challenge, and levels that reused an idea were cut because "by the third time, they would expect more" ([GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/how-astro-bot-went-from-tech-demo-to-playstation-superstar); [CGMagazine](https://www.cgmagonline.com/interviews/team-asobi-nicolas-doucet-astro-bot)). **Difficulty is never a setting**: "the difficulty has to come from the content that is on the main path, versus the content that is optional", the first level is "a toy box" that a very young child can play, and when platformer fans called the game too easy the team "cranked up the challenges on the side" ([Video Games Chronicle](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/features/astro-bot-how-team-asobi-has-turned-playroom-into-a-galaxy-sized-sequel/); [CGMagazine](https://www.cgmagonline.com/interviews/team-asobi-nicolas-doucet-astro-bot)). The density comes from process: single toys prototyped in isolation and reviewed every two weeks, about a third of the schedule spent prototyping, and about one brainstormed idea in ten taken as far as a prototype ([Edge](https://knowledge.edge-online.com/p/how-did-team-asobi-make-the-best-game-of-2024)).

### Rayman treats every object as a sign and every dare as a choice

Ubisoft Montpellier finished Rayman's controls before building levels, then spaced obstacles to his jump so that "the series of inputs required form a rhythmic tempo", because "humans can feel rhythm quite easily and apply it with little effort" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/remaking-i-rayman-i-camera-character-controls); [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/rational-design-the-core-of-i-rayman-origins-i-)). Each mechanic is taught in three stages, "exposition, validation and challenge": seen without being needed, tried "in a safe area" with "total freedom from time and danger constraints", then required. Readability is a code held across the whole game: "All platforms share a yellow-green color across all the different worlds", and "all of the dangerous gameplay elements share a similar spiky silhouette". Optional risk is announced before the player commits: a Skull Coin floating in view is "a clear indication of a dangerous and otherwise risky challenge", while two more are hidden on each level for "the explorer" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/rational-design-the-core-of-i-rayman-origins-i-)). *Legends* signals its hidden Teensies through three channels at once: foreground scenery that fades to show a passage, cries for help that the player hears nearby, and a row of slots ordered by location, so that an empty slot says where to look ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/secrets-design-in-rayman-legends)). A world runs in a fixed order of rising levels, a chase, a boss and a music level ([God is a Geek](https://godisageek.com/2013/08/rayman-legends-review/)). The music levels were laid out on "a music sheet which the level designers would be able to use as a grid" ([Designing Sound](https://designingsound.org/2017/12/06/rayman-fun-sound-music-an-interview-with-francois-dumas/)), and they stay in time only because "if the player doesn't run at full speed, the game is over" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/the-musical-box-27-game-music-level-design)). The chase that suits a gentle game is the one in which a treasure chest runs away: "The player feels a drive to catch the chest that he does not feel while being pursued by a wall of flames" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/rational-design-the-core-of-i-rayman-origins-i-)). There are no lives, and Michel Ancel's standing rule was "We have to always be welcoming to the non-players" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/the-evolutionary-process-of-building-i-rayman-origins-i-)).

### Celeste cuts the line into rooms and hides its kindness in the controls

*Celeste* keeps a tiny moveset, gives each chapter its own objects, and builds the chapter from short rooms: "Checkpoints occur at the start of each room", so failure costs seconds and each room holds one small idea ([Game-Wisdom](https://game-wisdom.com/analysis/celeste)). Its strawberries are the purest optional layer in the set. There are 175, found in almost every room and reached by a harder series of jumps, and they "serve no gameplay purpose other than as an optional challenge"; some set a rule instead of a place, such as the winged one that flies off if the player dashes ([Celeste Wiki](https://celeste.ink/wiki/Strawberries); [Tadeáš Jun](https://www.tadeasjun.com/blog/2d-level-design/)). The game's precision is cushioned by leniencies the player never sees, which Maddy Thorson listed herself: "You can still jump for a short time after leaving a ledge", a jump pressed just before landing still fires, and a clipped corner is corrected instead of punished ([Maddy Makes Games](https://maddymakesgames.com/articles/celeste_and_forgiveness/)). Assist Mode was renamed from "Cheat Mode" because of "that judgmental feeling", with the aim that players float between difficulty levels "without judgement" ([Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/celeste-difficulty-assist-mode)), and the composer chose to "keep the pace of the music lower in order to not stress the player out too much" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/road-to-the-igf-matt-makes-games-i-celeste-i-)). One critic's warning sets a limit: each chapter's twist runs out before its rooms do, and later chapters "could have removed a few rooms without the player noticing it" ([Game-Wisdom](https://game-wisdom.com/analysis/celeste)).

### Braid holds one rule still, and Portal shows how to teach it without words

Each *Braid* world has one rule of time, held fixed "for long enough that it can be explored before you go pulling the rug out" ([The Witness blog](http://the-witness.net/news/2011/11/designing-to-reveal-the-nature-of-the-universe/)). Jonathan Blow found the puzzles by asking the rule a question ("What happens if I gave the player the chance to rewind time in an unlimited way?"), testing it against every object in the game and keeping the best answers: "my role was just about knowing what questions to ask of the system", with perhaps 20 to 30 per cent of what he tried cut ([MCV/Develop](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/blow-braid-designed-itself); [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/indiecade-inside-jonathan-blow-s-puzzle-design-process); [The Witness blog](http://the-witness.net/news/2011/11/designing-to-reveal-the-nature-of-the-universe/)). The game promises "no filler" and no death, so that "the challenge is about solving puzzles, rather than forcing you to replay tricky jumps" ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/26800/Braid/)), and it almost never blocks the way: a stuck player can "play the rest of the game and then come back to that later" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/jonathan-blow-the-path-to-i-braid-i-)). Surprise comes only after an expectation has been honestly met: in World 4 every carried key is exempt from the rewind until the last puzzle's key is not ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/indiecade-inside-jonathan-blow-s-puzzle-design-process)). Blow's reason for simplicity is that "the clearer and simpler the puzzle is, the more beautiful and strong that feeling of epiphany can be" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/-i-the-witness-i-modeling-epiphany)). He refused a hint system on principle, which is the one place Elof's game should part from him. Valve supplies the teaching method Blow leaves implicit: a "very clean room" for the first use of each tool; then a gate "in which a solid understanding of key gameplay concepts is required"; one new idea at a time, because a room that "introduced too many new concepts at once" failed in testing; one unchanging visual cue per technique; and the plain finding that "without some serious prompting, players will rarely look up" ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2010/03/17/thinking-with-portals-making-a-test-chamber); [Portal developer commentary](https://theportalwiki.com/wiki/Portal_developer_commentary)).

### Unravel is the nearest relative: loved for its place, faulted for its puzzles

Coldwood's *Unravel* is a small figure in the northern Swedish outdoors, and its levels include a garden, a forest, a mire and a mountain. "Every location in Unravel is based on Northern Sweden", chosen because the places "have some kind of memory connected to them", and smallness is the source of the play: "when you're that small, even the simplest thing can become like a very interesting obstacle" ([EA](https://www.ea.com/games/unravel/amp/news/unravel-game-environments); [CGMagazine](https://www.cgmagonline.com/interviews/coldwood-follows-their-passion-with-unravel); [GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/an-interview-with-martin-sahlin-the-man-behind-the-delightful-unravel)). Yarny has about six verbs, and the levels get their variety from one or two behaviours of the place: waves and a crab that ferries him, a nut that grows into a snowball as it rolls, wind he must hold on against ([Gamerwalkthroughs](https://gamerwalkthroughs.com/unravel/chapter-2-the-sea/); [Gamerwalkthroughs](https://gamerwalkthroughs.com/unravel/chapter-9-winter-sun/); [Gamerwalkthroughs](https://gamerwalkthroughs.com/unravel/chapter-11-last-leaf/)). The garden level reads as a template: a reward for the very first interaction, one verb per beat, about fourteen beats on the main path, some ten ordinary objects (a sundial, apples in a pool, an armchair, a bike) that are each both scenery and puzzle, five secrets on short detours, a last puzzle that combines the verbs, and an event to end on ([Gamerwalkthroughs](https://gamerwalkthroughs.com/unravel/chapter-1-thistle-and-weeds/)). Its secrets follow a few repeatable rules: behind a plant that can be pulled aside, below the path, above it by an extra swing, back the way he came, and by using a puzzle object a second time ([Gamerwalkthroughs](https://gamerwalkthroughs.com/unravel/chapter-1-thistle-and-weeds/); [Shacknews](https://www.shacknews.com/article/93078/unravel-all-secret-collectibles-guide)). Martin Sahlin's favourite moment was when "you link lasso swings together...you get this flow of motion going" ([CGMagazine](https://www.cgmagonline.com/interviews/coldwood-follows-their-passion-with-unravel)), and **swinging was what reviewers enjoyed most in the hands** ([PlayStation LifeStyle](https://www.playstationlifestyle.net/review/464987-unravel-review-shooting-star-yarn-ps4/)).

The criticism is as instructive, and it is consistent across outlets (Metacritic 81, 78 and 75 by platform; [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unravel_%28video_game%29)). The same puzzle came back all game, "create a bridge between two points, move an item over this bridge, progress" ([The Curb](https://www.thecurb.com.au/unravel-review/)); nothing new arrived later, only harder jumps ([Push Square](https://www.pushsquare.com/reviews/ps4/unravel)); loose physics meant "needing to stack a block just so" ([GameCritics](https://gamecritics.com/brad-gallaway/unravel-review/)); floating footing in the mire sank after about a second ([Outcyders](https://outcyders.net/review/unravel-review-a-stitch-through-time)); a calm-looking game left one reviewer's inputs "frantic and stressed" ([Expert Reviews](https://www.expertreviews.co.uk/games/ps4-games/1404485/unravel-review-beautiful-but-straightforward)); usable things "don't stand out from the background enough" ([GameCritics](https://gamecritics.com/brad-gallaway/unravel-review/)); the yarn limit forced backtracking that one review called "the game's worst aspect" ([Game Revolution](https://www.gamerevolution.com/review/unravel)); a one-off kite ride was never taught ([PlayStation LifeStyle](https://www.playstationlifestyle.net/review/464987-unravel-review-shooting-star-yarn-ps4/)); and "there's no hint system built in" ([Outcyders](https://outcyders.net/review/unravel-review-a-stitch-through-time)). Coldwood's own summary of the lesson was "test more" and "plan more", for "a better learning curve without [difficulty] spikes" and pacing where "there's always something happening" ([EA](https://www.ea.com/ea-originals/news/unravel-two-director-interview-development)). *Unravel Two* added a guiding spark, three-step hints, slow motion and more checkpoints, and moved the hard material into separate challenge levels; reviewers found it smoother and still complained of "long stretches of just walking between puzzle areas" ([Vooks](https://vooks.net/unravel-two-switch-review); [Gamereactor](https://www.gamereactor.eu/unravel-two-review/)).

### Limbo and Inside use few pieces and answer every attempt

Jeppe Carlsen built *Limbo*'s puzzles in two passes, first as the player's "worst enemy", then "as your friend, so that you can give them the right clues", and held one rule above the rest: "It's very important that the correct solution is fairly easy to execute", because "if the player can't make the correct approach work, after a few times, they will stop trying that correct idea" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/gdc-europe-i-limbo-i-s-carlsen-on-making-players-your-worst-enemy-and-your-best-friend)). The team reduced each puzzle by asking "What is really necessary for this puzzle? What can we throw out?", threw away "70 percent" of what it made, and watched about 150 first-time testers in silence ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/hanging-in-limbo)). When testers failed a puzzle because they did not know an object could be pulled, Playdead added an earlier, easier puzzle that shows the pull, in place of the difficulty options its investors had suggested ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limbo_%28video_game%29)). *Inside* measures a puzzle at "around 100 meters or 1-3min of gameplay", streams 250 sub-scenes with no loading screen, and marks its episodes with sound instead of title cards ([Playdead](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1dew0TynVmtQf8OMLEz_YtRxK32a_0SAZU9-vgyMRPlA/edit); [The Computer Games Journal](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40869-018-0071-x)). Its animators' rule is the one a no-death game most needs: "Alle forsøg skal belønnes med en animation og et hint til hvad den korrekte løsning er", **every attempt is rewarded with an animation and a hint at the correct solution** ([Playdead](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1QN99FZUQl3KE4c7eg9-GLLEl3Og0FqreJbiTrCc8ZAk)).

The picture is made readable by subtraction: early scenes were "literally just fog + silhouettes", effects were turned "down 90%" with one moment in the game turned "up to 11", and the boy's head and spine turn toward things of interest, a hint channel with no interface ([Playdead](https://github.com/playdeadgames/publications/blob/master/INSIDE/rendering_inside_gdc2016.pdf); [Playdead](https://github.com/playdeadgames/publications/blob/master/INSIDE/turn_it_down_90pct.pptx); [Playdead](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1QN99FZUQl3KE4c7eg9-GLLEl3Og0FqreJbiTrCc8ZAk)). Secrets run against the direction of travel. *Limbo*'s first egg is reached by running left at the very start, and *Inside*'s thirteen hidden orbs lie back, down, up or behind something pulled away, each reached with verbs already known and many announced by a yellow cable, with a board of lights that counts them ([Gamer Guides](https://www.gamerguides.com/limbo/guide/collectible-locations); [TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/inside-hidden-orb-locations-guide/); [Gamepressure](https://www.gamepressure.com/inside/achievements-and-hidden-ending/z69058)). Walkthroughs also show scene functions that need no fear: a flock of chicks that gathers round the boy and follows him to a machine, bodies that copy the player's movement, a marching line to fall into step with ([Gamepressure](https://www.gamepressure.com/inside/farm/z3904f); [Gamepressure](https://www.gamepressure.com/inside/factory/zf9051)); no developer statement on their intent was found. What does not transfer is the feedback channel, since Carlsen called *Limbo* "a learning-by-dying game" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/gdc-europe-i-limbo-i-s-carlsen-on-making-players-your-worst-enemy-and-your-best-friend)). Arnt Jensen's own regret is a warning for the last third of a chapter: the second half of *Limbo* lost its scripted encounters and became a lonelier run of puzzles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limbo_%28video_game%29)).

### Little Nightmares makes scale the level and pays for depth

Tarsier casts a child in "a world that was made for other people", where she survives by "hiding, sneaking, and seeing things differently" and ordinary furniture is the terrain ([PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/2016/09/22/introducing-little-nightmares-on-ps4/)). The studio calls its play "hide and seek" instead of stealth, gives each area one pursuer with one defining trait, cut a whistle mechanic to keep the verbs few, and kept the game short on purpose ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Nightmares); [Push Square](https://www.pushsquare.com/news/2017/03/hands_on_little_nightmares_is_primed_for_greatness); [Game Informer](https://www.gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2017/05/30/little-nightmares-afterwords.aspx)). Dave Mervik says the scale difference lets challenges "feel natural" ([GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/little-nightmares-interview-into-the-maw)), and that is the test worth borrowing: an obstacle works when the child can name it and already knows how it behaves. Reviewers criticise exactly what a children's game cannot afford. Jumps are misjudged because "the depth made the next platform look like it was on a different jumping line" and the camera "feels like it's constantly swaying" ([EGM](https://egmnow.com/little-nightmares-review/)); a hazard strikes with "no warning it's there" and checkpoints are "just that little bit too far apart" ([Outcyders](https://www.outcyders.net/review/little-nightmares-review-small-scares-cheap-deaths)); chases and stealth in the sequel involve "some trial and error" and lose their force "through sheer repetition" ([NME](https://www.nme.com/reviews/game-reviews/little-nightmares-2-review-2875979)); and the sequel's companion "would take too long to take a cue" ([Shacknews](https://www.shacknews.com/article/122653/little-nightmares-2-review-terror-in-the-big-city)). Mervik admits that "the balance between fast pace and platforming versus slow pace and stealth has been a big challenge" ([GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/little-nightmares-interview-into-the-maw)).

### Ori is a guided line that reads as a world

Nibel is one seamless map with three self-contained dungeons, and it feels open because of four devices, none of which needs a large map. The player is shown the lock before the key: the first path is blocked by thorns that Ori cannot yet remove, and pickups are placed "just out of reach" until a later ability ([Ori Wiki](https://oriandtheblindforest.fandom.com/wiki/Sunken_Glades); [Design Delve](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y8UwWtxdy6s&t=247s)). Each ability is handed over in a pocket that cannot be left without using it; Mahler calls the double jump "one of the best tutorials" because "we let you drop into an area that you can't get out of", with no words beyond the button prompt ([scasm](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z7Krin-dvRE&t=2663s)). Gennadiy Korol gives the order: "We actually tease the new ability before we introduce it, then after we introduce it we teach it and then you escalate the challenge" ([TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps-switch-interview/)). World events re-read the whole map at once: all water is harmful until the Ginso Tree is restored, after which "the water in all of Nibel has been cleansed" and old lakes become new places ([Ori Wiki](https://oriandtheblindforest.fandom.com/wiki/Dangers_%28Ori_and_the_Blind_Forest%29); [Eurogamer](https://www.eurogamer.net/ori-and-the-blind-forest-review)). And the way back pays, since new powers "are almost guaranteed to help you discover a few hidden power-ups and secret areas along the way" ([IGN](https://www.ign.com/articles/2015/03/09/ori-and-the-blind-forest-review)).

The Ginso Tree is the model for a contained level. Brown describes a first half played without Bash, then the same kinds of challenge repeated with it, then a combined test and a set piece, so that the dungeon shows "what you can do without it, what you can do with it" ([Game Maker's Toolkit](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VQ_KrRq4UiA&t=472s)). Bash is the signature move because it reverses meaning: "Projectiles and enemies, which [were] once something to be avoided, are now a key part of your platforming, combat, and puzzle-solving skill set" ([Game Maker's Toolkit](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VQ_KrRq4UiA&t=214s)). The escapes that end each dungeon are the divisive part. They last under a minute with no checkpoint, and a reviewer who respawned 308 times in one playthrough called them "trial-and-error design" ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/playing-ori-and-the-blind-forest-on-switch-is-a-little-1838639709); [Polygon](https://www.polygon.com/2015/3/9/8180595/ori-and-the-blind-forest-review-xbox-one-PC)). Moon added checkpoints on Easy in the Definitive Edition and, in the sequel, made escapes easier to read and possible to abandon ([Ori Wiki](https://oriandtheblindforest.fandom.com/wiki/Ginso_Tree); [Game Informer](https://www.gameinformer.com/review/ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps/ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps-review-even-better-than-the); [GamesRadar+](https://www.gamesradar.com/au/ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps-is-taking-serious-inspiration-from-zelda-with-its-new-focus-on-fluid-combat/)). Chris McEntee's rule of thumb is the usable one: "like 60, 80 percent of the escape has to kind of flow naturally", a player should fail "once or twice" and never "five times or ten times", and every surprise "has to be react-able" ([Game Maker's Toolkit](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gIdHTL18kTU&t=909s)). On secrets he insists on a visible tell, "a little lip" at the edge of a secret wall, because "we want people to find secrets because we want them to feel smart" ([Game Maker's Toolkit](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gIdHTL18kTU&t=573s)). The studio dropped player-placed checkpoints for frequent automatic ones after playtests in which "nobody missed it" ([Stevivor](https://stevivor.com/features/interviews/soul-link-ori-will-wisps-thomas-mahler)). It designs in grey: "over a year and a half just honing the controls", then paper, a polygon blockout and "harsh and honest feedback" before any art, with Mahler policing "fun per inch" so that there is "never a moment" of five seconds of plain running ([Film Stories](https://filmstories.co.uk/gaming/ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps-interview-making-a-masterpiece/); [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-designing-the-gorgeous-metroidvania-i-ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps-i-); [scasm](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z7Krin-dvRE&t=2053s)).

### Cocoon corrects without words and without a fail state

Carlsen's later game is controlled "using only the left stick and one button" and "never explicitly explains anything to the player"; each room is designed "to be as accessible as possible for the player to make their own discoveries" ([Push Square](https://www.pushsquare.com/features/interview-how-cocoon-melds-many-worlds-into-one-promising-game); [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-challenges-of-laying-worlds-upon-worlds-in-puzzle-game-cocoon)). The first stretch is play with no puzzle at all, and after that the puzzles are ordered "so that each one is always only slightly more complex than the previous". Because a wrong idea cannot be punished, it must be answered: "Each wrong attempt at solving a puzzle needs to very clearly give the feedback that the solution did not work, that it never will work (with repeated attempts), and why it won't work" ([Remap](https://remapradio.com/articles/a-house-of-cards-built-on-trust-puzzle-design-in-cocoon-2/)). A short musical motif plays when the player finds a new use of the core idea, "a little confidence boost" before the result is visible ([Playday](https://playday.one/2023/12/23/creating-cocoon-developer-interview/)). The process is the opposite of gentle. Carlsen starts "way, way too hard" and invites testers, and an early two-switch room, "literally the simplest thing in the world", needed the most iteration of all ([GGRecon](https://www.ggrecon.com/articles/secret-shelf-cocoon/); [GoNintendo](https://gonintendo.com/contents/26996-one-of-cocoon-s-easiest-puzzles-was-the-hardest-for-the-devs-to-create)). He now asks of each puzzle "why is this puzzle here, what is its purpose?" ([PreMortem Games](https://premortem.games/2024/09/18/cocoon-creator-jeppe-carlsen-i-never-compromise-on-playability/)). A critic describes gates that rise behind the player in each puzzle area, so that the answer is never somewhere else ([TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/play-cocoon-without-guide-inside-limbo/)); Carlsen's own words on that device were not found.

## Ten principles for Elof's chapters, ranked by payoff

The ranking puts first what does most to turn a corridor into a place for the least new ground, given what the plan already contains; the last three mostly confirm the plan and tune it. In the last column, *firm* means several independent developer statements agree, *scaled* means the device is well evidenced but its size for a fifteen-minute chapter and a seven-year-old is this report's inference, and *thin* means the support is one critic, one walkthrough or reasoning from neighbouring evidence.

| 2 | Few verbs, the lace in every chapter, one rule of the place | Swing rings in all four chapters; each chapter's rule gives the lace and the rail objects a new job; rows of rings after each puzzle | Blow, Doucet, McEntee, Brown on the Ginso Tree, Sahlin, *Unravel*'s critics | Firm on the rule; the lace gap is read from the chapter files |

### Principle 1: one toy per arc, taken through four steps

Cut each chapter into **three arcs of four to five minutes, each owning one toy and ending at a big candy**, and within them seven to ten beats of one to three minutes. The four steps are Hayashida's, and five further developer accounts supply the detail. The toy is first seen in use, by the ghost or an animal, before it is needed: McEntee's "exposition" at Ubisoft and Korol's "tease" at Moon. Its first use is over flat ground where a miss costs not even a bubble, with a candy only that action reaches: Valve's "very clean room" and McEntee's "validation". Straight after comes a gate that only the new action passes, so that nobody wanders on without having understood. The third use changes one thing; the fourth combines the toy with something already known, because a new skill plus a second new element "proved to be too much for most playtesters" of *Portal* ([Portal developer commentary](https://theportalwiki.com/wiki/Portal_developer_commentary)). The arc ends on an easy, good-looking use that lands on the big candy, the job Mario's flagpole does ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/mario25th/2/4/)). McEntee adds that the escalations of one element should be interleaved with others, not run back to back ([Game Maker's Toolkit](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gIdHTL18kTU&t=507s)). Each beat should be framed so that the whole challenge, including where it ends, is in view before the child acts; Nintendo's lesson is to "always try to put the next step somewhere that's visible" ([Yamamura's Dojo](https://www.mariowiki.com/Yamamura%27s_Dojo)), and *Celeste*'s rooms are the same rule made into a structure. The minute counts are scaled: five minutes per idea is a critic's timing of 3D Mario, one to three minutes per puzzle is Playdead's figure for *Inside*, and fourteen beats per half-hour level is a count from an *Unravel* walkthrough. Gården already takes the swing through the first two steps, over flat ground at x = 54 and over the gully at x = 63.5, and then stops; the twist and the show of mastery are what is missing on the main path.

### Principle 2: few verbs, the lace in every chapter, and one rule of the place

*Braid* gives each world one rule and holds it still, *Unravel* at its best gives each level one behaviour of the place, *Astro Bot* cut levels that reused a toy without adding to it, and Rayman's designers set "significantly interesting and deep mechanics that are well explored" against "one-shot gameplay mechanics to feign depth" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/rational-design-the-core-of-i-rayman-origins-i-)). *Unravel*'s critics show the failure from both sides: one bridge puzzle all game, and nothing new later. The application is **one rule of the place per chapter, and the same small verb set asked a new question by it**. The built chapters already imply their rules: cones that roll and weigh in Granskogen, firm and soft ground and a carried light in Myren, gusts that come in beats on Berget. Gården's rule should be the lace itself. What the chapters do not yet do is carry the lace forward, and the Ori evidence says this is the largest single gain available: a signature move earns its place by making old kinds of room read differently, and the Ginso Tree's order (without it, with it, then a test) is how a chapter proves that. Each later chapter should hang rings in view before the lace can use them, then give the swing one new job taken from the chapter's rule. Three examples, offered as starting points: a ring on a branch that a cone's weight pulls down within reach; a ring over a soft tussock, so that swinging is the way not to stand on it; a ring in the lee of a boulder that is safe to use only between gusts. After each stopped puzzle, two or three rings at equal spacing give the "flow of motion" that Sahlin and his reviewers valued; McEntee's point about rhythm suggests, by this report's reading, that equal spacing is what makes the second and third swing feel like a repeat of the first. Objects on rails should return the same way, each with a job it has not had: a step, then a weight, then a stopper. Blow's method produces the candidates: list "what happens if" for the chapter's object against every verb, keep what makes a tester smile, and expect to cut a quarter.

### Principle 3: coil the line, and make every pocket let out forward

A ribbon gains places by going over, under and back, not by forking. The devices are all described at room scale in the sources: short offshoots from a linear spine (Stout); a route that climbs away from a landmark and later comes back to it, as when *Super Metroid* returns the player to the start with the music changed ([Game Maker's Toolkit](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nn2MXwplMZA&t=696s)); a gate opened from the far side that becomes a permanent short way back, as in *Hollow Knight*'s first zone ([Game Maker's Toolkit](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7ITtPPE-pXE&t=1780s)); and spaces above and below the path, as in *Unravel*'s tree climb and rappel and in the first Mario, which was planned around "land, sea and air" with underground sections as well ([Gamerwalkthroughs](https://gamerwalkthroughs.com/unravel/chapter-1-thistle-and-weeds/); [Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/0/3/)). McEntee's remark that merely "catching the existence of the path in the corner of their eye provides a sense of depth, coherency and expansiveness" means a second route need only be seen ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/rational-design-the-core-of-i-rayman-origins-i-)). For Elof this becomes **six to eight pockets of one or two screens per chapter, one landmark loop, one far-side shortcut, and two or three places where a high road and a low road part and rejoin at a big candy or a family crossing**; a small clearing with two or three short spokes that return to it is a further option. The four optional return loops already built are the first pockets of this kind. The counts are suggestions, not findings.

The limits come from research on children and are firmer than the counts. Children of six and eight learn landmarks before routes and do not yet treat a landmark at a turn as special, and not all six-year-olds could learn a route even with landmarks ([Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4304237/); [Journal of Environmental Psychology](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/95750)); more than one navigation scheme is "very confusing" for children ([Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/childrens-websites-usability-issues/)). So: one fork at a time and never a fork inside a fork; one unmistakable object at each fork; a branch whose rejoin point is visible from where it leaves; and pockets that end in a slide, a drop or a ride that rejoins the trail ahead, because "a dead end says 'Stop!' while a loop says, 'Go!'" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/loopy-design-encourages-longer-play-sessions)). A fall from the high road should land on the low road, and "up" should mean the richer, harder route every time; a path-by-path count of the first *Sonic* found an upper route that was slower and empty, which teaches a child nothing ([Grim in Pink](https://griminpink.substack.com/p/a-quick-and-dirty-path-analysis-of)). One engineering note follows from the data format: because the simulation's ground is a single line, a route that runs above or below another over the same stretch needs standable ledges of the kind the forest's challenge steps and the bog's tussocks already are.

### Principle 4: show it before it can be reached, and change the place once

Ori's openness is made of time as much as space. The Level Design Book makes the first device a rule, "Show the lock before the key", then "Remind the player about the lock when they get the key" ([The Level Design Book](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/layout/typology/gates)), and in *Metroid Dread*, by Brown's count, for 13 of 22 abilities "the lock is just around the corner from the key", still in working memory ([Game Maker's Toolkit](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5pop-cc9kmY&t=331s)). For a child with weak route memory that means the first place a new thing is needed lies within about two screens of where it was found, and is the same spot he was shown earlier. A ring hung over a gap a screen or two before the lace can reach it, with a sweet beside it, is the seven-year-old's version of Ori's unreachable pickup: something to point at and say "not yet". New rides and items are best handed over at the bottom of a soft one-way slide whose only exit is one safe use, Mahler's "best tutorial". Then, **once per chapter, one action about half-way through should change two or three screens already crossed**, and the way on should pass back through them: Ori's cleansed water, *Wonder*'s course that transforms in place, *Tropical Freeze*'s cart sawn into a boat. A family member's help is the natural cause, and the Kirby team's rule explains why the cause should be seen: "If the scenery around you moves or changes, it's not because of some magical force" ([Nintendo](https://www.nintendo.com/us/whatsnew/ask-the-developer-vol-4-kirby-and-the-forgotten-land-part-3/)). *Wonder*'s art director adds the condition that makes a transformation fair: the changed state needs its own look, because "keeping this feeling of trust in a side-scrolling 2D Mario game was crucial" ([Nintendo](https://www.nintendo.com/us/whatsnew/ask-the-developer-vol-11-super-mario-bros-wonder-part-1/)). A chapter can also open on what the last one left behind, as the storm level leads into the fire level. The devices are well evidenced at full size; their miniature versions have been tested on no player.

### Principle 5: pace by density and contrast, and let the big moment follow the ghost

Two clocks run in *Astro Bot*, and both scale down: something worth noticing about every thirty seconds, which the designer of the first Sonic stages also gives as the length of a short goal ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/game-design-psychology-the-full-hirokazu-yasuhara-interview)), and a new kind of play about every five minutes in a fifteen-minute chapter. Valve's rule is that such things are triggered by distance, never by a clock ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-cabal-valve-s-design-process-for-creating-i-half-life-i-)). The cheapest thirty-second item is a thing that reacts, because children "mine-sweep the screen" and try everything ([Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/childrens-websites-usability-issues/)). Mahler's "fun per inch" supplies a test the robot can run: **flag any stretch where Elof only runs for more than about five seconds with nothing to watch**. Contrast matters as much as density. Nintendo's lessons say "having downtime in your course creates contrast" ([Yamamura's Dojo](https://www.mariowiki.com/Yamamura%27s_Dojo)); a study of *Inside* warns that seamless play with cheap failure creates "a perpetual state of engagement" that tires the player unless lulls are designed in ([The Computer Games Journal](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40869-018-0071-x)); Blow alternates hard and easy because steady escalation is "kind of exhausting" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/jonathan-blow-the-path-to-i-braid-i-)); and Valve found that fatigue set in unless slow puzzles were broken up with "a much simpler task under time pressure" ([Portal developer commentary](https://theportalwiki.com/wiki/Portal_developer_commentary)). So each arc ends in a rest at a big candy on a safe perch that overlooks the next stretch, each chapter has one ride whose control is found in a few safe seconds, and each has exactly one moment turned "up to 11".

The plan's exciting sequences are that moment, and the evidence sorts them. E4, gusts announced a second ahead with boulders to shelter behind, is built like *Inside*'s shockwave section, whose cover is "close enough to be reached within one shockwave cycle" and whose sound keeps running through a failure ([The Computer Games Journal](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40869-018-0071-x)). E2, cones rolling from behind, is a pursuit, the kind Rayman's designers use to raise "the player's input frequency" and Ori's critics called trial and error. It stays fair under McEntee's rule that most of it flows from what is on screen, with every cone seen and heard a beat before it arrives; the chapter file already turns the camera so that the cones are seen. It would be gentler still as a chase: the cone Elof nudges already rolls away ahead of him, and making that cone, or the ghost on it, the thing he is trying to catch gives the drive McEntee describes without anything at his heels. E3 is the one to worry about and is taken up in the next section. In all of them the music should carry on through the glitter bubble, which is one of the three reasons Brown gives for why Ori's first escape works ([Game Maker's Toolkit](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VQ_KrRq4UiA&t=368s)), and the ghost should wait.

### Principle 6: easy to finish, with sweets for the brave and the curious

Nintendo, Retro, Ubisoft, the *Celeste* team and Team Asobi all put the difficulty in optional content and keep the critical path on core moves; *Sackboy*'s main path needs only "run, jump, slap, roll, grab" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-exploring-the-triumphant-level-design-of-i-sackboy-a-big-adventure-i-)). Two kinds of optional reward recur, the visible dare (KONG letters, Skull Coins, strawberries) and the hidden find (Puzzle Pieces, Teensies, orbs). So **two of a chapter's four sweets should hang in plain sight above a hard move or on the challenge route, and two should sit behind a tell**, low and to the side where a wanderer goes. Nintendo's own anecdote is of a younger son who found a hidden goal "precisely because he was wandering around down below" while his father and brother climbed ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/2/1/)). The plan's four hiding places (behind leaves, under roots, at the end of an optional swing, on the challenge route) already match *Unravel*'s grammar, and Gården, Granskogen and Myren already put a sweet behind the start, where *Limbo*, *Kirby and the Forgotten Land* and *Donkey Kong Country* put their first ([Gamer Guides](https://www.gamerguides.com/limbo/guide/collectible-locations); [WiKirby](https://wikirby.com/wiki/Mission_%28Kirby_and_the_Forgotten_Land%29); [Super Mario Wiki](https://www.mariowiki.com/Jungle_Hijinxs_%28Donkey_Kong_Country%29)).

The sources add four rules. The tell is constant: one sign used for nothing else, as *Inside*'s yellow cable is, or McEntee's "little lip". The first secret teaches the tell and cannot be missed: a reviewer of the Ori sequel missed a basic ability hidden near the start because he "hadn't expected anything so fundamental to be hidden in the tutorial area, where everything else was unmissable" ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps-review/)). A secret is fair when it has a visible clue, can be reached, has a way back and costs nothing to try, and no sweet should ask for a drop onto a floor he cannot see ([Yamamura's Dojo](https://www.mariowiki.com/Yamamura%27s_Dojo); [Stevivor](https://stevivor.com/2015/03/review-ori-and-the-blind-forest/)). And what remains is shown: four slots in path order at each big candy and on the chapter card, so that an empty slot says roughly where to look, as the Teensy display and *Inside*'s board of lights do. On a replay the helper can act as finder, as the parrot Squawks does for Puzzle Pieces and as *Kirby* does by revealing one hidden mission per completed run ([Super Mario Wiki](https://www.mariowiki.com/Squawks); [WiKirby](https://wikirby.com/wiki/Mission_%28Kirby_and_the_Forgotten_Land%29)). Nothing the story needs may depend on a sweet, the one thing *Braid* gets wrong by requiring every piece for its ending ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/on-game-structure)). The entrance to the challenge route needs one constant sign with its prize in view; Miyamoto observed that players "go out of your way to choose a tricky route" and then fail "no matter how many times you try it", which makes a level seem harder than it is ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/0/4)).

### Principle 7: candy that speaks in two voices and never lies

The trail is a wordless instruction the child wants to follow, and its makers state its rules. It traces the arc of a good jump so that moving well collects it; "You learn those jumps and get better", says the director of *New Super Mario Bros. Wii* ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/2/5/)). It always means safe, so a hazard never follows a candy or a landing. It is never shown where it cannot be reached, because a player "may be convinced there's a way to hit it and spend their entire day trying" ([Yamamura's Dojo](https://www.mariowiki.com/Yamamura%27s_Dojo)). It leads down blind drops: Nintendo's sky levels were saved by coins hung in the air to coax a jump that "wouldn't normally cross your mind" ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/1/5/)), and the pattern study warns against collectibles that lead over an edge whose landing is out of view ([Khalifa, Silva and Togelius](http://akhalifa.com/documents/level-design-patterns.pdf)). And it weakens with overuse: "If you place too many Coins in your course, they'll lose their power" ([Yamamura's Dojo](https://www.mariowiki.com/Yamamura%27s_Dojo)).

Two changes follow for Elof. **Thin the rows on flat ground and pay out clusters at destinations**: the top of a climb, the inside of a pocket, the end of a row of rings. The plan lays a candy every 1.5 to 3 EL so that each is visible from the last. That serves a child who fears losing the way, the players Nintendo added arrows and fences for, and it spends the power the Dojo warns about; the middle course is an unbroken trail on arcs and wherever the way is in doubt, and a sparser one across plain ground. And **give tempting candy a different look from trail candy**, so that "this is the way" and "this is extra" are different words. The Level Design Book ranks a line of pickups among the strongest guidance there is, far above a distant landmark, so a detour drawn in trail candy reads as the route ([The Level Design Book](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout/wayfinding)). Two smaller suggestions are this report's inference and are not stated in any source: put the last candy of a swing's arc at the right moment to let go, and stop the trail at the door of a puzzle scene and start it again at the exit, so that it says where and never how.

### Principle 8: three pieces, the sweet in sight, and every wrong try answered

The accounts of what a good puzzle is made of agree to a degree that is rare in design writing: a goal visible at once, a catch that makes it look impossible, very few parts, one insight, and an execution that is short and easy once the idea is found ([Juha-Matti Santala's notes](https://notes.hamatti.org/gaming/puzzle-game-design); [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/gdc-europe-i-limbo-i-s-carlsen-on-making-players-your-worst-enemy-and-your-best-friend)). For Elof: the big candy, a sweet or the next crumbs of the trail are in view across the obstacle before any tool is shown; the object, its rail and its destination fit one camera frame; and no puzzle has more than three things to act on. The rails already answer *Unravel*'s worst complaint, and they need stops that objects settle into, so that "nearly right" becomes "right"; Ron Gilbert's rule is to "figure out what the player is trying to do" and, if it is what the game wants, "help the player along and let it happen" ([Grumpy Gamer](https://grumpygamer.com/why_adventure_games_suck)). The plan's puzzle state machine, with its commit point, local reset and return route, already covers his rule against dead ends. A wrong placement should fail by a lot, since a half-working wrong idea leaves players "very attached to trying that idea". Because no fall can say "wrong", **each puzzle scene needs authored answers to its two or three likeliest wrong attempts**, an animation and a sound that show it did not work and why; this is Playdead's rule and Carlsen's rule for *Cocoon*, and it is the largest piece of new authoring the research asks for. *Cocoon*'s motif suggests the matching reward: a short sound when he performs the key step, before the result shows. Carlsen's warning carries extra weight for small hands, since a right idea that fails twice in the doing is abandoned, which makes generous swing and jump windows a matter of puzzle design. On the trail a crossing should accept two arrangements (a step or a swing), which protects against being stuck on the designer's one answer; for sweets and the challenge route it should be strict. That is the mix Frozenbyte describes for *Trine 4*, "many open-ended puzzles" beside "the stricter type" that needs a specific ability ([Wccftech](https://wccftech.com/trine-4-hands-on-interview-e3-2019/)).

### Principle 9: replace what death supplied

In *Limbo* death does three jobs: it says "wrong", it says "try again from here", and it adds tension. The glitter bubble and the big candy take the second, authored answers take the first, and the third is the one *Epic Yarn* shows cannot be left empty. For a child who plays games made for eleven-year-olds the danger is the first *Epic Yarn* build, easy and pretty and dull after two stages, far more than the thorny one. The sources offer substitutes that cost nothing on a miss. **Things to do well**: the director of *New Super Mario Bros. Wii* says players feel "the greatest pleasure when they think, 'I've gotten good at this!'" ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/2/6/)); Erikson's stage for these years is competence against inferiority ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erikson%27s_stages_of_psychosocial_development)); and a six-year-old in a usability study dismissed a site as "for babies" ([Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/childrens-websites-usability-issues/)). A second half that pushes back: Shinya Kumazaki aims for "ease in play in the first half and sudden increased tension in the second half" ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/kirby-dream-land/0/3/)). Anticipation in place of threat: wanting to catch the ghost, wanting to see what the machine does. And a mark of pride for the challenge route on the chapter card, with its absence never shown as a failure; Nintendo put a medal on the title screen for players who never called up the hint block ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/0/5/)). One choice is open. Retro's letters and Rayman's Skull Coins are forfeited on a failure and Puzzle Pieces are kept on touch; the plan's rule that no candy is ever taken away argues for keeping all four sweets on touch and letting the route itself be the dare. The warning is first-hand and firm. The remedies are a mapping onto this game.

### Principle 10: fail softly, forgive the hands, and help only when called

The plan's rules here are the ones the evidence supports, so this principle is tuning. The bubble should set Elof down at the doorstep of the scene he fell from, on still ground, facing it, with every part of it in view: *Odyssey*'s assist bubble carries Mario "to the last solid ground" ([Super Mario Wiki](https://www.mariowiki.com/Assist_Mode)), and the reason given for the co-op bubble in *New Super Mario Bros. Wii* was that a weak player who returns "in a difficult spot" will "just mess up again" ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/2/0/)). Checkpoints should be predictable and visible before the hard part, as in the Ori sequel, where "if you reach a ledge free of threats, it's likely a checkpoint" ([Game Informer](https://www.gameinformer.com/review/ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps/ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps-review-even-better-than-the)). Thorson's forgiveness list transfers almost whole: coyote time and jump buffering, which the plan has; a ledge grab that pulls him up when he nearly makes it, in place of corner correction; a generous catch radius and release window on rings; and momentum carried off moving things ([Maddy Makes Games](https://maddymakesgames.com/articles/celeste_and_forgiveness/)). If the same swing is missed twice, widening its window does the job of *Unravel Two*'s slow motion without a menu. The plan's rule that no move needs more than the stick and one button fits what is known of small hands: fine motor skill and coordination are "limited" at six to eight, and shoulder buttons are "simply out of reach for the typical 8-year old" ([Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/children-ux-physical-development/); [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2013-07-10-giant-dualshock-knack-accessible-lessons.html)).

The helper's three steps (look, point, show) match Nintendo's Super Guide, *Unravel Two*'s tiered hints and Sesame Workshop's three levels of prompt for preschoolers ([Sesame Workshop](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/SesameWorkshop-2012.pdf)). The sources add four refinements. **The helper reads the state of the puzzle and never points at a step already done**; the authors of the one large study of hints for children suspect theirs failed because the hints "did not adapt to the student's partial solution" ([O'Rourke, Ballweber and Popović](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/orourke2014hsm.pdf)). It keeps still and silent while he works, since Valve found that players "really, really don't want to listen" while they are thinking ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2010/03/17/thinking-with-portals-making-a-test-chamber)). After the third step has shown the whole solution, the pieces slide home and he does it himself, which keeps the doing and part of the insight: one designer's distinction is that eureka can be shared by watching, while the pride of beating something hard cannot ([Diplograph](https://diplograph.net/notes/games/puzzle-design/eureka-is-not-fiero)). And a demonstration is played "slowly and calmly", the Nintendo rule being "Play considerately" ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/2/3)). Help should be free and uncounted, because children hoarded hints they had to earn ([O'Rourke, Ballweber and Popović](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/orourke2014hsm.pdf)). It should also wait to be asked: Miyamoto settled on eight failures before the hint block appeared, because at three his reaction was "Leave me alone!" ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/0/5/)).

### Two habits under all ten: readable at a glance, and grey first

Rayman's designers hold that every object is a sign, and the faults reviewers found in *Unravel* and *Little Nightmares* are mostly failures of seeing. The rules that follow are few. One sign per meaning, used for nothing else: the lace's red for the lace, the rings and the handles on things that move, which in a northern forest means keeping lingonberries away from that red. A handle on everything that moves, since *Unravel* marked its tie points with a sparkle and a critic still wanted a handhold to "glitter more brightly" ([GameCritics](https://gamecritics.com/brad-gallaway/unravel-review/)). A ring that lights when the lace can reach it, and only one ring in reach at a time, because Yarny would "latch on to the wrong object" ([Paste](https://www.avclub.com/unravel-review)). Elof and every usable thing readable as silhouettes with a contact shadow against a quieter, fogged background, which is how *Inside* is lit. Every jump and swing on the side-view plane with the camera still, and a camera that leads a swing so that the landing is in frame before he lets go; one *Unravel* reviewer called a fixed camera "silly" when "you can swing from one place to the next" ([Stuff](https://www.stuff.tv/?p=353123)). Team Asobi's order is "Contrast is our top priority" ([CGMagazine](https://www.cgmagonline.com/interviews/team-asobi-nicolas-doucet-astro-bot)).

Every studio here builds play before art and cuts hard. *Limbo* had "no graphics or sound in it" for a long time; Moon takes a level from paper to a blockout and blunt review before any artist touches it; Nintendo builds the middle courses first, moves their easier features forward and makes the first screen last ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/hanging-in-limbo); [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-designing-the-gorgeous-metroidvania-i-ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps-i-); [Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/1/4/)). Sahlin found *Unravel*'s beats by walking a wire-and-yarn doll through real woods and photographing it ([GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/an-interview-with-martin-sahlin-the-man-behind-the-delightful-unravel)), a method that works for a 15 cm figure in the real garden, with the pictures kept as uncommitted reference. Testing means watching in silence: "Sit down and watch people play your game. Don't just have them send you reports", says Kim Swift of *Portal*, and Hayashida reads "the expressions on people's faces" instead of questionnaires ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/best-of-gdc-the-secrets-of-i-portal-i-s-huge-success); [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-structure-of-fun-learning-from-i-super-mario-3d-land-i-s-director)). *Cocoon*'s simplest room needed the most iterations, so each chapter's first puzzle deserves the most testing. With one adult tester before Elof plays, the robot has to measure what it can, as Playdead's recorded playthrough did on daily builds ([Playdead](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1dew0TynVmtQf8OMLEz_YtRxK32a_0SAZU9-vgyMRPlA/edit)): time per beat, seconds of plain running, and bubbles per segment set against a fail budget. One designer's scale, from his own test, is one failed attempt in ten for an easy jump, two for a medium one and six for a hard one ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-rational-design-handbook-an-intro-to-rld)); holding the main path to the first figure and the challenge route to the second is this report's suggestion. The robot can also hunt for bypasses in teaching scenes. Valve closed a shortcut with a glass wall when testers skipped the lesson a room was built to give, and let one stand "where skipping ahead arguably takes more skill than solving the puzzle properly" ([Portal developer commentary](https://theportalwiki.com/wiki/Portal_developer_commentary)).

### A fifteen-minute chapter, cut the way these sources would cut it

The table is a synthesis, a check to hold each chapter's own beat outline against, and its minutes are scaled from sources that write about longer levels and older players.

| Minutes | Beat | What happens | Pattern taken from |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 to 1 | Toy-box arrival | Nothing to fail; the first things touched react; a sweet behind or above the start; the landmark and the chapter's end in view | *Astro Bot*, *Limbo*, *Super Mario 3D Land* |
| 1 to 5 | Arc one | The chapter's toy seen, tried on flat ground and gated; a ring or sweet shown that cannot be reached yet | Hayashida, Valve, Ori |
| 5 | Rest | A big candy on a perch overlooking the next stretch; the four sweet slots shown | Yamamura's Dojo, the Ori sequel |
| 5 to 9 | Arc two | A climb away from the landmark; the toy with one thing changed; a pocket with a sweet that lets out forward; a row of rings for flow | *Unravel*, Stout, Sahlin |
| 9 to 10 | Ride | Control found in a few safe seconds; cannot fail; a sweet inside it | Rayman's change of play, *Unravel* |
| 10 to 11 | Family crossing | A rest; the helper's act changes two or three screens already crossed | Ori's world events, *Wonder* |
| 11 to 13 | Arc three | Back across the landmark's top; the twist and the combination; the far-side shortcut opens; the challenge route's marked entrance | Hayashida, Brown on *Metroid* and *Hollow Knight* |
| 13 to 13.5 | Breather | Quiet, pretty, safe; a big candy | McEntee, Khalifa |
| 13.5 to 14.5 | Exciting sequence | Thirty to forty-five seconds of the chapter's own moves, following the ghost; the music runs through the bubble | Rayman's chest, McEntee's rule |
| 14.5 to 15 | Landing | An easy flourish to the last big candy; a glimpse of the next chapter | Mario's flagpole, *Hollow Knight* |

Three items in the current plan sit on the wrong side of this table. **E3, the sinking tussocks, is the plan's nearest copy of the most criticised passage in *Unravel*, in the same landscape**: floating moss in the mire where "hanging around for more than a second or so drops poor Yarny into the water" ([Outcyders](https://outcyders.net/review/unravel-review-a-stitch-through-time)), part of the stretch a German review singled out for "frustrierenden Trial-&-Error-Passagen" ([ComputerBase](https://www.computerbase.de/artikel/gaming/unravel-test.53539/seite-2)). The bubble, the firm island with its big candy and the *Lugnt* setting already remove most of the cost that made it hateful, so the case for keeping it rests on building it as a rhythm crossing: pale tussocks at equal spacing so that the hops fall into a beat, a sink that is slow, visible and audible, and nothing sinking on the main path outside that short run. It deserves the most test time of any sequence. The lace bridge between two rings, which the plan calls "Unravel's trick", is the verb behind the puzzle that critics met "over and over again" ([VideoGamer](https://www.videogamer.com/reviews/unravel-review/)); it is safest used once or twice in the whole game, and never as bridge, drag, progress. The soft glint on usable things is the marker *Unravel* relied on and a critic asked to be brighter; the plan's floating verb icon and the red ring already do more, and the evidence argues for a visible handle on everything that moves.

## Where the evidence is thin, and where the sources disagree

**No source in this study tests a platformer with seven-year-olds.** The child evidence is three usability studies of how 125 children aged 3 to 12 use screens ([Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/childrens-websites-usability-issues/)), two wayfinding studies that are not about games, a guide for preschoolers, and remarks about reach and coordination. Nothing was found on how many branches or how long a detour children of this age tolerate, on checkpoint spacing, on how children respond to collectibles placed on and off a path, or on whether hints change a child's enjoyment. The one large study of hints for children ran in an educational puzzle game with about 50,000 students, and all four hint designs slightly lowered performance against no hints (the group without hints won the evaluation level 77.82 per cent of the time, the hint groups 75.52 to 77.01), with concrete hints beating abstract ones; it measured levels completed, not pleasure ([O'Rourke, Ballweber and Popović](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/orourke2014hsm.pdf)). The staged helper therefore rests on consistent practice at Nintendo, Coldwood and Sesame Workshop and not on proof. Developer statements about "non-players" and beginners concern adults, so every judgement here about what suits Elof is an inference from them.

Every number here is borrowed or invented. Thirty seconds, five minutes, the 60 to 80 per cent of a set piece that should flow, eight failures before a hint and the one-in-ten fail rate all come from games for general audiences, the last from one designer's own test whose players are not described. The five-second limit on plain running is Mahler's remark about Ori, a much faster game. Six to eight pockets of one or two screens, two or three high and low roads, and the minutes in the chapter table are starting values with no source behind them. *Unravel*'s length was clocked by reviewers at anything from three to ten hours, so the figure of one beat every two minutes is rough. Only the robot's timings and Elof's first play can set these.

Several sources could not be read in full. Game Maker's Toolkit videos are quoted through their descriptions, press summaries or captions; the quotations from Mahler's 2026 interview, from Design Delve and from Brown's *Tropical Freeze* video come from automatic captions, so their wording is close paraphrase. The reading of *Tropical Freeze*'s two collectibles is Brown's, with no first-hand Retro statement behind it. The *Unravel* reviews in Eurogamer, IGN, GameSpot, Polygon and Edge were seen only as one-line aggregator excerpts, and nothing was found on Coldwood's greyboxing or testing beyond "test more" and "plan more". Playdead has published no talk on *Inside*'s camera or on why its secrets are hidden as they are, so the intent behind its scenes is inferred from walkthroughs. No Tarsier statement on staging a chase was found, *Cocoon*'s closing gates are a critic's description, Blow's IndieCade talk is known through press reports, and *Yoshi's Crafted World* is described from a wiki. Many quotations in the underlying notes were extracted by an automated page reader and should be checked against the linked page before being reprinted in public. The remarks about the built chapters rest on the plan's sections on play and on the chapter files' data, not on playing them.

The sources also disagree, and the disagreements mark choices and not errors. Brown praises Ori's first escape for having no checkpoints; Moon added them on Easy and softened the sequel, which makes the rule a matter of audience. Blow refuses hints, Miyamoto accepts them late and uneasily, and Ron Gilbert built a hint book that "actually knows where you are in the game", because the alternative is a walkthrough on the web ([Inverse](https://inverse.com/gaming/return-to-monkey-island-interview-pax-west-2022)). One-way drops shrink the space a player must hold in mind and cost exploration. The Level Design Book warns that heavy guidance makes a level "feel like Disneyland" ([The Level Design Book](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout/wayfinding)), while Nintendo added arrows and fences for players who feared getting lost; for a seven-year-old the main path should take Nintendo's side and the side paths can stay subtle. Collectibles are kept on touch in one tradition and forfeited on failure in another. Frozenbyte wants several solutions and Blow wants none found by chance. In each case this report has chosen the side that costs a child least, and a tester's face is the way to find out whether that was right.

## Conclusion

The ribbon is the asset, not the obstacle. Moon Studios accepts areas with one entrance so that it knows which moves the player has at every step; a left-to-right chapter gives that knowledge away free, and the effort saved on branching is best spent on returns. A useful measure of a chapter is therefore not how many obstacles it holds but how many things in it are met a second time, changed: a toy with a new job, a place after its event, a ring that could not be reached before, a sweet slot that points back. By that count Granskogen's cones score three and Gården's lace scores two on the main path, and the cheapest first step in this report is to hang rings in the three chapters that have none.

Most of what the research asks for is authored reaction, not terrain: answers to wrong attempts, a helper that reads the puzzle, a ghost that waits, a gag every half minute, a place that changes when a family member helps. That is animation, sound and flags on ground that already exists, which suits a project whose chapters are in greybox. It also sets the terms for the first time Elof plays. Every child-related number here is a guess until then, so that session is the experiment: watch his face in silence, count bubbles and helper calls per beat, note where he looks first and which correct idea he gives up on, and expect to cut. Playdead threw away 70 per cent and Blow a quarter; a chapter that keeps everything built for it has not yet been tested.
